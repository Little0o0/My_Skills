# Research Paper Writing

Produce a paper argument, not a research-management report. Lead with the problem, why it matters, the precise gap, and what the method establishes. Keep the Chinese brief and manuscript aligned in claims, notation, evidence status, and structure.

## Preferred manuscript structure

The following is this user's writing preference, **not a universal conference requirement**. A later user instruction overrides it. Check the actual venue template for mandatory formatting and statements.

1. **Introduction** — motivation, concrete gap, method overview, and contributions. Introduce the contribution early rather than making readers wait through a survey.
2. **Preliminary and Challenge** — only the necessary background; formal objective, constraints, notation, and prediction/information model; the shared workflow of a relevant frontier method class, illustrated by representative baselines; its common unresolved problem in a specified setting and why it matters.
3. **Method** — organize subsections around how contributions address the challenges. Define mechanisms and state theoretical results with their assumptions and core conclusions in the main text. Keep essential proof ideas when they explain the mechanism.
4. **Experiment** — organize by claims or research questions. Specify comparisons, metrics, information and compute budgets, and interpretation. Separate completed evidence from proposed experiments.
5. **Conclusion** — state the established contribution and its implications within the demonstrated scope.

Place the full **Related Work** survey in the appendix, together with detailed proofs and supplementary experiments. This does not prohibit citations or the explanations of direct baselines needed to understand the main argument. Keep sufficient attribution and positioning in Introduction and Preliminary and Challenge.

Honor venue-mandated statements in the positions and formatting required by the template; keep them unnumbered where permitted rather than adding a sixth numbered scientific section. If the venue explicitly requires otherwise, follow that requirement and explain the narrow structural exception. Do not invent current venue policies.

## Preferred Introduction progression

For this user's Introduction plans and drafts, follow the six paragraph roles below; the separate analysis paragraph is optional, so the final Introduction may contain five or six connected paragraphs. Identify the actual paper problem and its consequences before assigning content to these roles:

1. **Motivation and entry point (aa → bb → cc → dd → ee).** Connect progress in the field to a concrete practical bottleneck and the relevant technical approach. Explain only the background limitation needed to introduce the baseline class. Keep this motivation distinct from the problem the paper actually solves: a broad quality, cost, or scalability tradeoff must not silently become the claimed contribution. Prefer declarative prose; do not end the background with a rhetorical question that enlarges the paper's scope.
2. **How the baseline class works (gg addresses ee).** Explain the common mechanism through concrete objects and actions: what is processed, what reference or objective is used, what changes, and what benefit this provides. Establish these objects before referring to an abstract problem, unit, or state. Mention workflow properties in proportion to their explanatory role; do not end this paragraph with an abrupt pivot to the proposed contribution. By this user's preference, organize it around a method class with grouped citations, not named algorithm examples or a paper-by-paper list. Put member-specific differences in Preliminary and Challenge or Related Work.
3. **The problem and its consequence (xx).** Open with an explicit turn and one compact challenge statement that a reviewer can understand on its own. Then explain when the limitation matters and what undesirable outcome it permits or produces. A missing version of the proposed score, module, or decision rule is not sufficient as the problem. Keep the analysis procedure and new metric definitions for the following paragraphs. Support a class-level gap through the shared mechanism or representative evidence; separate one method's observed degradation from a general failure claim and from its proposed explanation.
4. **Analysis of that same problem (yy, optional).** State how the problem is examined and what this analysis identifies: for example, a controlled comparison, decomposition, or examination of reachable changes. Give the short diagnostic conclusion that explains the design, keeping indispensable scope visible. Do not merely repeat the problem or introduce an unrelated second limitation. For multiple components, connect each diagnosis to the same stated problem. When a short analysis does not help, merge the necessary intuition into paragraph 3 and retain the formal work in Method. Do not call an untested explanation of observed degradation an established cause.
5. **Design derived from the analysis and its result (zz).** Start from the preceding diagnosis. For each component, explain which identified limitation motivates its design, what it changes, and what capability or supported result follows. Introduce names after the reader understands their purpose. Keep solver branches, detailed state inventories, and formal derivations in Method; do not substitute an implementation list for this reasoning. Place contributions here without restarting the motivation or overstating the evidence.
6. **Evidence.** State which comparisons test the claims and the actual principal findings. In a plan or an unmeasured draft, describe the proposed experiments; do not turn an example of completed-result prose into fabricated findings.

Keep ee and xx distinct so the story advances. Do not invent an integrated SOTA workflow, a universal ranking, or a shared prediction model to fit paragraph 2. Check the actual representative works behind the grouped citations and acknowledge generic methods that reproduce the computations; synthesis must preserve substantive differences even when individual names are absent from the prose. Broadening the wording or renaming one algorithm as a class neither establishes a shared gap nor proves originality. Do not imply that prior methods fail in their own setting merely because the paper studies an extension.

Check the argument across paragraph boundaries, not just compliance with the paragraph count. Read the topic sentences consecutively: the baseline must address the background limitation; the problem must concern that baseline; the analysis must examine that problem; each design choice must answer a finding from that analysis. Adding “However,” “Further analysis,” or “Based on this” does not repair a change of subject. If a second component cannot fit this chain, resolve the scope mismatch explicitly rather than forcing it into a purported root cause.

Before finalizing, verify that an unfamiliar reader can state the problem and its consequence without naming the proposed method, then explain why the analysis motivates each component. Keep problem evidence, diagnostic reasoning, and design choices in their respective roles. When revising from comments, update conflicting claims and paragraph roles in the brief as well as the prose; retaining an obsolete outline can reproduce the same failure in the next draft. Generalize the demonstrated writing failure into the skill, not the current paper's terminology or an obligatory domain-specific opening.

Check the intervention implied by the problem statement: what direct remedy would a reader expect, and does the proposed analysis explain why this method addresses that problem? Hold competing changes fixed to test the connection. Sharing a broad outcome such as poor task quality is not enough to connect unrelated analyses. Prefer one specific, defensible argument over widening the problem to accommodate every candidate component; a weak extension can remain outside the Introduction's main contribution. Preserve the strongest rationale for the baseline when stating what remains unresolved.

By this user's preference, keep the Introduction free of formulas, including inline equations. Explain mechanisms in words and refer to figures when they make the causal steps clearer; bibliographic citations still support prior-work claims. Move training operators, gradient projections, solver branches, and other formal details to the appropriate body section. A planned figure is not evidence until its stated content exists.

Treat the Introduction as a compressed account of the body, with a two-way correspondence: every introductory claim has a specific body location, and every main contribution in the body is previewed in the Introduction. Paragraph 1 motivates the whole paper; paragraphs 2–3 summarize Preliminary and Challenge; paragraphs 4–5 summarize Method; paragraph 6 summarizes Experiment. Conclusion returns to the supported conclusions rather than introducing new claims.

Preliminary and Challenge supplies task definitions, the mechanism-defined class gg and its shared workflow with representative examples, and the supported common trigger and consequence of xx; it develops the same class and scope previewed in paragraphs 2–3. Method develops the root-cause analysis yy, then connects yy → design → established result zz. Put counterexamples that establish the problem in Preliminary; put counterexamples used to derive the method's root cause or design in Method. Do not pre-prove the method in Preliminary. If paragraph 4 is omitted, the brief intuition can close paragraph 3 and lead into the method overview; the underlying formal analysis still belongs in Method. Experiment provides evidence for the respective contributions.

These are paragraph roles, not six subsection headings. Keep the broad background to one sentence and the full survey in the appendix. Summarize across sections rather than duplicating paragraphs; do not change targets or assumptions between the Introduction and body, or leave an introductory claim unsupported elsewhere. Synchronize the dedicated Introduction plan, Chinese brief, and LaTeX writing notes when these files exist.

## Files and figures

Use one independently editable `.tex` file per main section under `contents/`, included by the main manuscript. Keep figures under `figures/`; use method subsections for modules rather than multiplying top-level sections. Put appendices in separate content files as appropriate. Adapt to existing filenames and template conventions rather than replacing a working project unnecessarily.

Maintain the Chinese brief as an argument and experiment-design map, not a transcript of reviews. Put provenance, review exchanges, execution ledgers, and historical decisions in their own records. Do not leave unsupported placeholder results in manuscript figures or tables.

When the user wants an experiment section before results exist, draft the actual protocols now: the claim being tested, comparisons, controlled variables, metrics, and what outcomes would support or weaken that claim. Use planned/future tense where appropriate. Leave numerical result cells empty and label them as unmeasured; do not substitute zeros, invented trends, or completed-result language. Missing measurements do not prevent writing the experimental design.

## Academic prose and terminology

Write for a reviewer who knows machine learning but may not know the application area. Establish a concrete decision, what changes during computation, and why the existing prediction no longer answers the needed question before introducing specialized names. Explain each maintained quantity through the job it performs. Carry the motivating example into the method so that definitions and guarantees answer questions the reader already has.

Use natural, restrained academic prose. Develop each paragraph from an observation to its consequence and then to the next question. Avoid generic sentences such as “the contribution is the connection between X, Y, and Z”; say what is constructed, what can now be computed or reused, and which result supports that capability. The six Introduction roles guide the argument, not a visible template of stock transitions. Attractiveness comes from a consequential question and a specific answer, not stronger adjectives.

Separate the reading path from the audit trail. In the abstract and introduction, explain a result's meaning and indispensable scope; put its full parameter ranges and quantifiers with the formal theorem. Keep essential assumptions visible, but avoid interrupting every positive statement with a repeated disclaimer or a list of things the method does not claim. Explain strong alternatives and capability overlap where the comparison is developed. Do not substitute terminology consistency or reviewer approval for checking whether an unfamiliar reader can accurately restate the problem, mechanism, and contribution.

While drafting, prioritize explanations over a previous page estimate when the user relaxes the length limit. Do not remove causal steps, compress conditions into unreadable noun lists, or shrink typography to maintain that old target. Apply the actual submission limit during a later, explicitly relevant editing pass.

Maintain canonical terms and symbols across the manuscript, Chinese brief, figures, and captions. Define each technical term at first use, then use its established name consistently; do not vary terminology merely for stylistic variety. Preserve meaningful distinctions between a method class and its individual members.

Prefer connected prose to frequent `itemize`, `enumerate`, or `\item` blocks. Use lists when stating research questions or when explicit enumeration materially improves clarity; short parallel points may use inline numbering such as 1. and 2. Do not mechanically flatten a useful list into an overlong sentence. Use dash interruptions sparingly; LaTeX `--` and `---` produce en and em dashes, respectively. Avoid pervasive bold or emphasis; reserve it for method names or genuinely necessary emphasis, and preserve the venue's formal theorem styling.

## LaTeX source layout

By this user's preference, write prose as one paragraph per source line. If the user chooses one sentence per line, keep sentences in the same paragraph adjacent with no blank lines. Blank lines separate natural paragraphs; do not insert them merely to format source code. Do not use fixed-width formatters to wrap prose. Later user preferences override this default.

Preserve semantic boundaries in equations, align environments, lists/items, tables, and macro commands. Keep each logical comment on one line, and do not change the scope or newline-suppression behavior of `%` comments during reflow. Do not bulk-reformat vendor template/style files, math-macro libraries, or original reference backups. For a pure reflow, verify that rendered layout remains unchanged.

## Contribution and evidence discipline

Evaluate novelty at the level of the complete method: its input/output object, assumptions, dependency structure, supported reuse, guarantees, and comparison to the strongest fair alternative. Classical components, a short proof, or similar performance do not by themselves disprove overall originality. Conversely, renaming a composition or failing to find a precedent does not establish originality.

Give exact specializations when a generic framework can reproduce the method. Do not weaken prior methods to create a separation. Attribute known components, then identify what the paper actually constructs or proves. Distinguish correctness, novelty evidence, capability, explanatory theory, and performance; winning a benchmark is not the sole criterion for a theoretical contribution.

Keep assumptions adjacent to theorem claims. Distinguish the actual prediction target and moment/error model from posterior calibration or a learned representation. Do not imply a new training method, distribution-shift guarantee, or empirical benefit without corresponding evidence.

For experiments, align data splits, target selection, input information, randomness, budgets, and confidence intervals with each claim. Include strong same-capability baselines and representative coverage of the class discussed in paragraphs 2–3; retain member-specific information models and budgets instead of treating the class as one interchangeable algorithm. A claim about the class needs evidence spanning its relevant mechanisms or an argument that applies to the shared mechanism, not merely a comparison to one convenient member. Retain failures and incomplete cells. Describe author-reported results separately from reproduced results. Follow the scope and authorization already established in the session: writing work includes necessary source checking, source-document downloads, and document builds, but does not by itself expand authorization to new research experiments or training; preserve any explicit authorization already given.

## Experiment design and interpretation

Start with the claim, before choosing a dataset or statistic. State why the claimed improvement matters to the task, what comparison could establish it, and what a successful outcome would justify saying about the method. Check whether the proposed design can distinguish the method from a strong relevant alternative under the stated information and resource conditions. Design the experiment to make a real advantage convincing, rather than accumulating measurements around whatever the implementation already exposes. An advantage can concern comparable error, robustness, solution quality, computational cost, or a demonstrated capability; it need not be higher classification accuracy.

Distinguish the roles of comparisons. Evaluate the complete method against relevant external methods, including strong same-capability alternatives. Use ablations to explain which choices cause an observed benefit; removing a component from the proposed method does not replace an external comparison of its competitiveness. Choose baselines and regimes because they test the claim, not because they are likely to lose. Preserve matched information, tuning opportunities, compute accounting, and paired statistical units; separate comparisons with different assumptions or prediction budgets rather than treating them as a single ranking.

Choose primary outcomes that make the claimed advantage meaningful and comparable. Internal statistics such as intermediate variance, representation size, or diagnostic agreement should explain the main result, rather than become an isolated main experiment with no demonstrated connection to the task or claimed capability. A theoretical comparison of attainable error or robustness under aligned assumptions can supply substantive evidence without an accuracy benchmark. Put correctness self-checks, certificate validation, and invariant tests in the appendix or validation details, with a brief main-text reference when needed; passing them establishes implementation correctness, not a competitive advantage.

Write each experimental argument through four roles: (1) motivation—why this question matters to the task or contribution; (2) approach—the model, data, baselines, controlled variables, and protocol; (3) actual results—the measured comparison and its quantitative magnitude; (4) interpretation—how those findings address the motivating problem and support, limit, or contradict the claimed advantage. These are logical roles, not four mandatory headings or equally sized paragraphs. Routine sensitivity studies can combine motivation and approach. When the result does not reveal a separate mechanism, combine results and interpretation instead of adding a repetitive explanation section.

Quantify the result against the named reference and use the correct scale. Report the underlying values, whether larger or smaller is better, and the denominator for a relative improvement. For example, accuracy increasing from 70% to 75% is a gain of 5 percentage points, or about 7.1% relative to the baseline; it is not an unspecified “5% improvement.” Distinguish time reduction from speedup, retain the stated timing boundary, and report uncertainty using the actual independent experimental units. Highlight meaningful measured advantages while retaining ties, losses, failures, and relevant adverse regimes. Interpret diagnostic changes in relation to those findings; a favorable diagnostic alone does not establish improved solving, prediction, or runtime.

When measurements are unavailable, write the motivation and executable design, leave results explicitly unmeasured with TBD or an equivalent label, and state in advance how positive, negative, tied, or inconclusive outcomes would be interpreted. Do not invent magnitudes, trends, or favorable explanations. If a proposed measurement cannot support the intended claim even when it succeeds, revise the comparison or narrow the claim before running it. Experimental design and manuscript writing do not themselves authorize additional experiments beyond the session's existing scope.

## Review and revision

Follow the review process already requested in the session, including any specified local Codex review mode. When the session calls for Codex and real Claude review, preserve that division: obtain the actual available Claude tool response, record its scope, address substantive findings, and obtain a revision review when requested. Do not present simulated feedback or a different reviewer as Claude. If unavailable, report the specific blocker while completing independent authorized writing work.

This skill does not require a new multi-review cycle for every routine edit. Do not overwrite frozen review inputs, experiment artifacts, or historical decisions; preserve revisions as new snapshots and distinguish old evidence from current manuscript text. Reviewer approval is evidence about the reviewed scope, not certification of global firstness or acceptance.
